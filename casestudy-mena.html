Context

Plenty of design, no system

MENA Capital had regulatory approval and a backend trading engine, but nothing clients could use yet. Some screens had already been designed, spread across different files, with no shared colours, components or rules. Each new screen started from scratch.

I joined as the only designer, reporting to the founder. No PM, no design team, and requirements that changed week to week. The founder wanted screens fast. I knew that building more screens on a scattered base would only make the mess bigger.

So the job was really two jobs: pull the design into one place, then build the product on top of it, without slowing the team down.

Consolidating

One source of truth, grown from real screens

I went through the existing work, kept what was worth keeping, and set the foundations first: colour, type, spacing and radius, all as tokens. Then I started designing product screens on that base. Whenever a screen needed a new component, I built it once and added it to the system.

Tokens are named for what they do, not what they look like. Engineers pick color/semantic/error because it's an error, not because it looks like the right red. Changing the brand colour later means editing one value.

Foundations

170+ tokens across 5 collections: brand greens, a 6-step neutral ramp, 4 semantic colours, spacing and radius.

One typeface

Inter only, with 10 styles from Display to Label. Easy to read at small sizes, which matters on data-heavy trading screens.

Every state

Components cover default, hover, pressed, focused, disabled, loading and error, not just the happy path.

Patterns, not just parts

Login cards, sidebar navigation, data tables and dashboard widgets, ready to assemble into new screens.

How I worked with AI

Figma, code and Storybook, kept in step

The pace was high and I was the only designer, so I leaned on AI for the mechanical work. I connected Claude to Figma through a bridge plugin. It built token sets, swatch collections, the type scale and component states from decisions I had already made.

Sometimes the fastest route was straight to code. Claude built those components using the same tokens, and they went into Storybook at the same time, so engineers could see them right away. The rule never changed: Figma is the source of truth, and nothing goes into code that the system doesn't know about.

Design decisions

The calls that shaped the product

Account types belong in the app

My first version showed account types as a big pricing table, and the feedback was fair: it felt like the website had leaked into the product. The choice still belongs in the app, because clients open extra accounts after signing up and only the app knows their balance. So it became one field in the form, and the full comparison lives on the website.

Light mode by default

Stakeholders wanted a dark dashboard with gradients. On a platform full of numbers, tables and error states, light reads faster and makes problems easier to spot. I showed them the research, and dark mode stayed as an option instead of the default.

Plain forms for KYC

There was a push for decorative, branded onboarding forms. People are typing sensitive personal and financial details here, and every bit of decoration makes the form feel longer. I kept them plain, short and calm.

One extra step before money moves

Anything that can't be undone goes through preview, review, confirm, and destructive actions get their own red button. It costs one tap and saves people from the "wait, what did I just do?" moment.

The important numbers first

Screens open on the few figures people check every visit. Experienced traders can expand panels and pick their own columns, so new investors aren't buried and power users aren't limited.

Validation and outcome

Tested before engineering started

I tested the prototype with 6 participants across the three core flows: onboarding, deposits and transfers.

94% task completion

People created an account, made a first deposit and moved funds without help. All rated the usability as excellent.

3x faster screen delivery

With the system in place, new screens took hours to put together instead of days.

Zero overrides

Across 12+ screens in development, engineers never needed a one-off colour, spacing value or component.

Founder sign-off

I presented the test results with the reasoning behind each decision. The light-mode direction and plain onboarding were approved in the meeting.

Where it ended up

A system the team could keep using

When I left in July 2026, the first modules were being built against these designs. Because everything lives in one system, in Figma and in Storybook, the team can keep adding screens without a designer redrawing the basics.
